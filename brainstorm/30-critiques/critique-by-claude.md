## Cursor — Candidate 1: Speech AI Assurance Lab

- **Verdict:** SURVIVES (conditionally — the strongest of the five, but its central assumption is honestly flagged and still unresolved)
- **Evidence audit:** Mostly clean. DCB0129 safety-case spend by vendors is real, invoiced money (Assuric/Naq exist; R4 confirms £5–25k per safety case), and NHS revenue is genuinely conditional on it. But note the sleight of hand the proposal itself admits: the invoice that exists is for *safety-case documentation*, not *specialty performance evidence*. Nobody has yet paid a penny for "SLT-specific eval sprints." The ASSUMPTION that vendors will redirect compliance spend to specialty evidence before a buyer mandates it is the entire business, and it is stated-preference-shaped even though no clinician preference is cited — it's *inferred vendor* preference. The validation test (deposit from 20 named safety leads) is exactly right and genuinely falsifiable. Keep it.
- **Distribution audit:** Founder-led outbound to a finite vendor list is the rare channel a 2-person team can fully cover — R1 names the target list for free (splose, Heidi, WriteUpp, Jane, Ambiki, Beam, Agilisys, Invision360). The public benchmark is R3's rule 6 ("publish evidence as a channel") executed correctly. One gap: the proposal never says who *hosts* the benchmark's credibility. Without RCSLT/ASHA/academic co-signature, a vendor can ignore a two-person startup's scoreboard indefinitely.
- **Moat audit:** The clinician-graded failure corpus and longitudinal vendor results are a real data moat *if* engagements happen. Against a funded fast-follower (Pacific AI/John Snow Labs extending MedHELM into allied health — R4 names them), the defense is jurisdiction coverage (UK statutory frameworks) and first-mover benchmark citation, which is adequate but not deep. The bigger threat is the buyer simply not existing: R4's CHAI precedent — the US health-AI assurance-lab network that collapsed in early 2026 because deployers wouldn't pay third parties to vet vendors — is the closest real-world analogue and Cursor never mentions it.
- **Feasibility audit:** Two landmines under-weighted. (1) DCB0129 work requires a registered clinician as Clinical Safety Officer; neither founder is one (R4 flags this; the proposal doesn't). The design partner or a contracted CSO must be in the plan before the first sprint is sold. (2) Referee/player conflict: this candidate coexists in Cursor's own portfolio with Candidate 5's "vendor-sponsored training" — a rating agency taking money from the rated. That must be resolved by policy on day one, not hand-waved.
- **Steelman fix:** n/a (survives) — but add the CSO arrangement and a published independence policy as preconditions, and time the wedge to the DCB0129/0160 revision consultation (closes 11 Sep 2026 — respond to it, per R4, as free authority-building).
- **What the proposer missed:** CHAI's assurance-lab failure (sell to regulated vendors, never to deployers); R4's recommendation to fuse this with safety-case-as-code (O3), which converts "nice-to-have specialty evidence" into "condition-of-revenue compliance artefact" — the single move that de-risks the flagged assumption.

## Cursor — Candidate 2: Statutory Report Exchange

- **Verdict:** MAJOR REVISION
- **Evidence audit:** The demand money is real and admissible: UK tribunal reports at £600–£1,500 with deposits actually charged, and IDEA §300.502 genuinely obliges a district to fund an IEE or file due process — verified ([§300.502](https://sites.ed.gov/idea/regs/b/e/300.502)). But the applicable invoice is for the *clinician's report*, not for an *exchange*. No evidence is offered that anyone pays an intermediary 12–15% today; the proposal assumes the margin into existence. The US side is weaker than presented: parents get only one IEE at public expense per disputed evaluation, districts impose cost caps, location and examiner-qualification criteria, and publish approved-evaluator lists ([Wrightslaw/OSEP](https://www.wrightslaw.com/info/test.eval.choice.osep.htm), [CADRE](https://cadreworks.org/listserv-topic/independent-educational-evaluation-iee)) — meaning the district partly gatekeeps the panel, per state, per district.
- **Distribution audit:** SEND solicitors/advocates are genuinely open and near-zero-saturated (R3's channel map agrees, and this is its strongest feature). But Cursor ignored the free substitutes already occupying the match function: ASLTIP's "find a therapist" directory and The SEND List — which Cursor itself cites as a *source* without noticing it is a *competitor*. Why does a solicitor pay a case fee to a two-person startup instead of emailing a known expert from a free directory? The answer ("reliability, turnaround") is unproven.
- **Moat audit:** Weak. "Network liquidity" at a scale of five cases and three clinicians is not liquidity; the scarce asset is tribunal-experienced SLTs, and nothing stops a funded follower (or Mable Therapy, which already owns UK school relationships per R1) from signing the same small expert pool. Referral relationships with small law firms are semi-defensible at best (R3 rates them "semi").
- **Feasibility audit:** Serious landmines. (1) UK: a business introducing self-employed clinicians to hirers for a fee almost certainly falls within the Employment Agencies Act 1973 / Conduct Regulations 2003 — "employment" there expressly extends to self-employed professional engagements ([Gaby Hardwicke briefing](https://www.gabyhardwicke.co.uk/briefing-notes-and-faqs/employment-agencies-and-employment-businesses/), [Conduct Regs 2003](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2003/3319/contents)) — bringing EAS inspection, restrictions on fee structures, plus safeguarding/DBS and clinical-governance duties the proposal never budgets. (2) Expert independence: any fee arrangement giving the platform or expert an interest in case outcomes is incompatible with the expert's duty to the tribunal; contingency-type arrangements are effectively prohibited ([Academy of Experts](https://academyofexperts.org/practising-as-expert/expert-fees/contingency-fees/)). A flat intake/workflow fee survives this; a percentage "case fee" invites challenge to the expert's evidence — a catastrophic own-goal in litigation contexts. (3) Strategic: R4 explicitly warns that arming the parent side forecloses the LA-side statutory market (O1, the "biggest prize").
- **Steelman fix:** Invert the customer. Sell the *expert SLT* (starting with the design partner) a per-case statutory-report production workflow — secure intake, evidence bundling, statutory-compliance checking, audit trail — at £150–250/case flat, with solicitor relationships as demand-gen for the clinician, not as a brokered exchange. That is R4's O8a plus R1's "SLT-side tribunal report engine" (its top near-term pivot), keeps DevUp a software company, reuses A1/A2, and steps around agency regulation and expert-independence risk entirely.
- **What the proposer missed:** R1's implication 3 — the whitespace is the *therapist's evidence-grade report tool*, already identified as the strongest near-term pivot; and the existence of free matching substitutes inside its own citations.

## Cursor — Candidate 3: Speech Authorization Recovery

- **Verdict:** KILL
- **Evidence audit:** Partially smuggled. The ASHA Vermont survey (43% payment reductions, 59% access problems) is *stated experience*, not spend — inadmissible under PROTOCOL rule 1. The real invoice cited — RCM at 6–10% of collections — is for full-scope billing, and it proves the budget line belongs to *RCM firms*, not to a standalone denial-evidence vendor. The 82% MA overturn rate is plausible but cuts both ways: if appeals overturn that easily, the pain is effort, not expertise — a staffing problem RCM firms already solve.
- **Distribution audit:** The white-label channel premise checks out in general — white-label RCM partnerships are an established industry pattern ([AllegianceRCM](https://allegiancercm.com/why-white-label-rcm-partnerships-are-the-future/), [ZOLL](https://www.zolldata.com/healthcare-financial-data/rcm-resellers)) — but that pattern runs the *wrong direction*: back-office providers white-label *to* client-owning billing firms. DevUp would be the back office: commodity position, and the billing-firm partner sees every workflow detail and is the natural fast-follower. The channel gatekeeper is the predator.
- **Moat audit:** A one-state, one-payer, one-denial-class rule graph is weeks of work for any RCM firm with actual claims volume. Their data exhaust dwarfs anything a concierge service accumulates.
- **Feasibility audit:** The pricing model is a legal landmine the proposal never mentions: percentage-of-recovery compensation for billing/claims work on Medicaid claims is explicitly prohibited in New York and problematic in other states as fee-splitting, and the OIG has repeatedly refused safe-harbor protection to percentage arrangements ([NY Health Law Blog](https://www.nyhealthlawblog.com/2017/03/22/percentage-based-billing-contracts-violate-medicaid-regulations-and-may-constitute-improper-fee-splitting/), [OIG](https://oig.hhs.gov/compliance/physician-education/fraud-abuse-laws/)) — and Medicaid denials are the proposal's chosen wedge. Add: a UK-based two-person team selling US Medicaid services across timezones, state-variable rules, BAAs, and near-zero asset transfer (Cursor's own asset-leverage paragraph is a confession: US FHIR, auth, payer integrations, denial workflows all net-new; the harness only useful "after the concierge wedge proves recovery"). This discards essentially all four assets — which under CONTEXT rule 4 requires an extraordinary case, and this isn't one.
- **Steelman fix:** None within constraints. If the founders ever want this, it's a feature sold *to* an RCM platform, not a company.
- **What the proposer missed:** R2's reachability rule (50 qualified buyers in 30 days through channels you already touch — a UK team touches zero US clinic owners); R4's ranking, which puts every US-payer option below the UK statutory lanes precisely on feasibility.

## Cursor — Candidate 4: SLP Capacity Clearinghouse

- **Verdict:** KILL
- **Evidence audit:** The invoices are real and are the strongest argument *against* the idea. The DCPS contract cited is with **Amergis** — the rebranded Maxim Healthcare Staffing, a national staffing giant — visible in the cited filename itself. The Ealing £1.7M spot-commissioning spend flows through incumbent agencies and framework contracts. The evidence proves buyers buy capacity *from large, credentialed, payroll-carrying staffing firms via procurement frameworks* — i.e., the channel is owned, exactly what PROTOCOL rule 2 demands proposals disprove.
- **Distribution audit:** "Monitor public RFPs" as a two-person entrant against Amergis, Presence-class teletherapy staffing firms, and (per R1) newly funded delivery players like Parallel ($20M) and Mable is not an open channel; it's the most capitalized corner of the entire SLP market. Public-sector staffing additionally runs through approved-supplier frameworks a startup can't join quickly.
- **Moat audit:** "Liquidity in micro-blocks" fails on first principles: with a 21% SLT vacancy rate, *supply* is the binding constraint. A marketplace whose scarce side is clinicians competes for those clinicians against agencies paying payroll, benefits, and 25–40% markups while Cursor proposes to live on 10–15%. Any liquidity achieved is instantly poachable — both sides are named in public documents.
- **Feasibility audit:** This makes DevUp an employment agency/employment business in the UK (Conduct Regulations 2003, EAS inspection, safeguarding vetting duties for work with children) and a de facto staffing/credentialing operation in the US (state licensure verification, background checks, professional-liability exposure, worker-classification risk). Cursor's own asset paragraph concedes the AI layer "is not required" — so the pivot discards A1 entirely and most of A3's purpose. Two engineers running a regulated, low-margin labor marketplace is the single worst scope-to-team fit in either agent's portfolio. Noala (R1) died with $4M trying to run an SLT service business; this proposes it with £0.
- **Steelman fix:** None as a business. The only salvageable atom — "sell recovered capacity, not admin time" — is the framing thesis from CONTEXT, better served by O4-style triage tooling later.
- **What the proposer missed:** That its own headline invoice names the incumbent (Amergis); R3's rule 3 (first revenue loop must be card-payable — district and council procurement is not); R2 rule 7 (no durable asset accumulates: placements are transactions).

## Cursor — Candidate 5: AI Safety Simulation CPD

- **Verdict:** MAJOR REVISION (demote from pivot to wedge/funnel)
- **Evidence audit:** The ASHA course at $59/$77 is genuine revealed spend — admissible, and the best-evidenced consumer demand in Cursor's set. But the UK half is inflated: HCPC sets *no minimum CPD hours* — it requires a profile only if selected in the ~2.5% audit — so the "forcing event" for UK SLTs is weak (the proposal's own HCPC citation shows this). And the flagged ASSUMPTION ("buyers will pay more for simulation than lecture") is stated-preference logic reintroduced through the back door; the pre-sale test correctly disciplines it.
- **Distribution audit:** The CPD-partner channel is genuinely open (R3: "no AI vendor owns a CPD partnership"; Elklan's 100k practitioners prove the lane). Co-branding is also the right call, because going direct is slower than implied: ASHA Approved CE Provider status costs $1,750 to apply plus ~$975/year, with a 4–6-week review and ongoing compliance ([ASHA CE](https://www.asha.org/ce/for-providers/step1/)) — surmountable, but not week-two. The awkward fact: ASHA is simultaneously the accreditor, the channel, and the incumbent competitor selling the very AI-ethics course cited as evidence. A professional body can commission a me-too course and erase pricing power overnight — Cursor names this risk but doesn't price it.
- **Moat audit:** Weakest moat of the five. Course content is copyable in weeks; accreditation relationships are non-exclusive; "learner errors improve the cases" is a real but slow data loop. As a standalone business this is a content shop.
- **Feasibility audit:** Low regulatory risk (correctly avoids norm-table IP). The real hazard is portfolio-internal: taking vendor sponsorship for training while Candidate 1 grades those vendors compromises the benchmark's independence — the one asset that makes Candidate 1 work. One of the two must own the "independent" brand.
- **Steelman fix:** Reposition as the *distribution engine for the assurance play*: the "Can you catch unsafe clinical AI?" cohort is a superb owned-audience builder (R3 rule 1) and a clinician-grader recruitment pipeline for Candidate 1's failure corpus, plus modest cashflow. Drop "vendor-sponsored training" permanently. Run the pre-sale test as designed — it's cheap and protocol-compliant.
- **What the proposer missed:** HCPC's no-hours regime gutting the UK forcing event; the synergy (clinician graders, corpus growth) that makes this valuable as a subordinate wedge rather than a capped standalone.

## Portfolio-level observations

1. **This is a services-and-marketplace portfolio from a software team.** Four of five (1, 2, 3, 4) are operations businesses — evaluation services, a brokered exchange, contingency recovery, a staffing clearinghouse. Two engineers have demonstrated ability to build compliant software, not to run regulated labor/claims operations. Only Candidates 1 and 5 have a credible path back to product economics.
2. **Asset-leverage discipline collapses across the set.** Candidates 1 and 5 monetize A1 (the harness — the crown jewel per R1/R4). Candidates 3 and 4 discard nearly everything, triggering CONTEXT rule 4's "extraordinary or out" bar, which neither clears. Credit where due: Cursor's asset-leverage paragraphs are unusually honest about what's net-new (unclosed sign-off flows, unwrapped PHI routes) — that honesty is itself evidence the platform transfers less than everyone assumes.
3. **The highest-ranked lane in the research is missing.** Nothing addresses the UK LA-side SEND statutory market (R4's O1, "biggest prize") or safety-case-as-code (O3, "best risk-adjusted") except obliquely via Candidate 1. Worse, Candidate 2 as written would *foreclose* the LA lane by siding with parents against LAs. The synthesis must not let Cursor's framing silently discard the memos' top-ranked options.
4. **Internal conflicts need resolution before synthesis:** (a) referee/player — Candidate 1 grades vendors; Candidate 5 takes vendor sponsorship; Candidates 2/4 make DevUp a clinical-services operator whose own AI would need third-party assurance; (b) geographic — Candidate 3 is US-only from a UK base, Candidate 2 splits focus across two legal systems in week one. Pick one jurisdiction for first revenue.
5. **Merge recommendation for P4:** Candidate 1 (assurance lab, fused with R4's O3 safety-case-as-code) as the core; Candidate 5 demoted to its audience/grader-pipeline wedge; Candidate 2 rebuilt as SLT-side statutory-report tooling (converging with R1's lane 3 / R4's O8a); Candidates 3 and 4 dead. That yields one coherent company — "the evidence layer for SLT AI and SLT statutory work" — instead of five unrelated ones.
6. **Process note:** Cursor's validation tests are the best part of the submission — every one demands real money and names a kill threshold. Whatever survives synthesis should inherit them verbatim.
